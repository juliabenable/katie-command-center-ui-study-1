WHAT WE ARE SHOPPING FOR

The Aug 18 call fixed the requirements. Katie needs a home page that is a prioritized to-do across all buckets ("here's the drafts that were flagged... the orders... the content that needs to be reviewed... as opposed to a Slack alert"). Flags must arrive with a pre-written draft. Ownership must be obvious. She must be able to take a thread over, and give it back. And per Brian, the hard problem is the bird's eye view: "how do we give Katie a bird's eye view of the millions of decisions... almost like a little stock trading dashboard." Every screenshot below is evidence for one of those needs.

1The queue that says what the AI needs

Podium · Zendesk · Missive · Gladly

Katie today: "I have to go review every single individual Slack channel... especially overnight or over the weekend... sometimes we miss them." The field's answer is a queue where the rows themselves announce the machine's state, and stuck conversations form their own bucket instead of hiding among handled ones.

In the command center: the home queue is bucket tabs (Flagged texts · Orders · Content review · Overnight ledger) where each row carries the agent's state chip and a clock, plus an ownership filter cloned from Podium: Assigned to agent / Assigned to Katie / Assigned to Des / Agent needs help. The overnight ledger is Gladly's table with our three outcomes: sent / flagged / held.

2The draft review ritual

Zendesk · Intercom · Missive · Chatwoot

The Aug 18 decision: "if they send a flag of, I'm not sure I should be sending this SMS. Here's what I've drafted." The converged industry pattern is a draft in a clearly bounded panel with exactly two verbs, and keyboard-speed approval.

In the command center: the flag card is the SMS bubble as it would send, two verbs (Approve · Edit), Tab/Esc keyboard flow, and any side effect (deadline moved, reminder suppressed, payment mentioned) as its own amber chip that Katie approves with the text, never hidden inside it.

3"Why I escalated," said in the thread

Gladly · Gorgias · Front

A flag without a reason makes Katie re-read the whole conversation. The best systems make the AI publish its escalation as a legible object: the reason, the suspect content, and a one-line summary. And crucially, giving up is a visible event, never silence.

In the command center: every flag card leads with a reason line written by the checker agent ("creator sounds frustrated", "mentions a product problem", "can't verify the deadline I want to reference"), the specific suspect sentence highlighted inside the draft, and a one-line summary. This is the UI face of the checker agent from the kickoff brief.

4Whose thread is it right now

Front · Zendesk · Chatwoot · Kustomer · Podium

Katie's fear is double-texting a creator the agent is mid-conversation with, or assuming the agent has something she actually owns. Every serious system makes ownership an explicit, visible state with a one-click takeover.

In the command center: each thread header states its owner (Agent · Katie · Des · Suppressed) with Front's step-in banner when the agent holds it, Zendesk's visual read-only treatment while it does, and Chatwoot's inline system chips as the permanent record. Stepping in pauses the agent's queued sends for that creator automatically.

5Take over without re-reading

Ada · Front · Gladly · Kustomer

The cost of an escalation is the read-in. The field's best trick is making the AI write the read-in for you: a living summary, the step trail of what already happened, and on close, a record of who did what.

In the command center: flag cards open with the agent-written read-in: one line of summary, the last thing the creator was told, and what the agent already checked. On close, the same mechanism writes the creator note automatically, which is exactly the note-agent behavior Nisarg described.

6The context rail

Missive · Gorgias · Front · Intercom

Approving a draft to a creator requires her campaign state next to the thread: stage, deadline, shipment, payment. Helpdesks solve this with a context sidebar; none of them can show campaign context, which is precisely Benable's advantage in building its own.

In the command center: a per-creator rail with stage, fork, deadline clock, shipment state, payment state, consent state, and the auto-notes ("prefers DMs", "away until Wednesday"), built as re-orderable blocks so Katie tunes it herself. Plus one ask-box wired to the same CreatorContext payload the agent uses, answering with citations into admin.

7Rules Katie can read (and test)

Gladly · Chatwoot · Intercom · Missive · Zendesk

The kickoff brief proposes escalation policy as plain-language playbooks. The field agrees, and adds two things: rules should wear their own usage stats, and there should be a way to test a rule against a message before it goes live.

In the command center: the playbook library is a card per scenario (away creator, product problem, angry creator, deadline ask), each card showing how often it fired this month and where it routed, with a test box at the top ("paste a creator text, see what the agent would do"). Katie edits cards, Brian's system compiles them.

8Where escalations land, and who gets pinged

Podium · Intercom

An escalation is only as good as its routing. The SMS-native answer (Podium) is to route per reason to named people, previewing exactly what the ping will look like. Intercom adds the missing middle mode between autonomy and handover: ask a human first.

In the command center: a routing table per escalation reason (product problem → Katie, brand complaint → Katie + Julia, task complete on a paid collab → Katie), each row previewing the flag card it will produce. The agent's escalation IS a note in the thread plus a row in the queue, exactly Podium's mechanic, so it works even before the dashboard exists.

9Clocks that chase you

Chatwoot · Front · Intercom · Podium

Katie's overnight and weekend anxiety is a deadline problem: which waiting creator breaches first. The field solves it with countdowns attached to the work itself, and pushes for what slipped anyway.

In the command center: every flag carries a reply-by clock (frustrated creator = short clock, routine draft = longer), the queue defaults to next-clock-first, and the 8am digest lists what breached overnight, which replaces scrolling every Slack channel back to Friday.

10The scoreboard

Intercom · Gladly · Gorgias · Zendesk

Nisarg on the call: "the biggest thing is gonna be abnormalities... detecting what's not normal is probably gonna be very important." The field's dashboards answer a calmer version of the same question: how much did the AI finish alone, where did it bail, and is it getting better.

In the command center: the scoreboard is the graduation dashboard from the kickoff brief: per-intent %-sent-unedited (the Superhuman metric), the outcome mix (sent / flagged / held), and an abnormality strip (send volume per brand per intent, STOP-rate, reply-rate) that would have caught the 28 Litsea dead-reminders bug in a day.

11Supervision that teaches

Gorgias · Gladly · Front · Kustomer

Katie's review minutes are training data if the UI catches them. The best systems put provenance and a one-tap verdict on every AI message, and turn a rejection into "here's what you should have used instead."

In the command center: every agent message shows which playbook and which facts produced it, Katie's edits are diffed and stored as new few-shot examples automatically, and rejections ask "which playbook should this have used?" That closes the loop the kickoff brief calls the maturity ladder, and it is how Katie stops babysitting.
RULES OF THE FIELD

What every good AI-supervision UI agrees on

  1. Ownership is a visible state, never an inference. Banner, badge, read-only treatment, and a one-click Step in (Front, Zendesk, Chatwoot).
  2. Escalations form their own queue and clean up after themselves. Podium's "AI Needs Help" filter with auto-clear is the reference.
  3. A flag always carries its reason, its draft, and a one-line summary. Gladly's handoff trio is the fullest version; silence is never a state (Front's give-up event).
  4. Review is two verbs and one key. Edit / Approve, Tab / Esc. Anything heavier stops being a ritual and becomes a chore.
  5. Side effects are chips, not sentences. Zendesk shows actions separately from the reply so approval is informed.
  6. The thread is the audit log. System lines for every assignment, rule and handoff, in sequence with the messages (Chatwoot, Kustomer, Missive).
  7. Rules are plain sentences that wear their own stats and have a test box. Gladly + Intercom + Chatwoot converge on exactly the playbook-cards idea.
  8. There is a middle mode between autonomy and handover. Intercom's "ask for input" under a confidence threshold is the draft lane, productized.
  9. Clocks live on the work. Same countdown on the row and in the thread; sort by next breach; push the overnight misses at 8am.
  10. The scoreboard is a funnel plus an outcome mix, with formulas printed on tiles and a per-reason graduation table (Intercom, Gladly, Gorgias).
  11. Corrections must teach. Provenance on every message, thumbs plus why, and "what should it have used instead" (Gorgias).
  12. Nobody shows campaign context. Every context rail here is commerce data bolted on. A rail with stage, deadline, shipment, payment and consent is the one thing no vendor can give Katie, and the strongest reason her command center is ours to build.
Where I would start. Prototype two screens first: the home queue (pattern 1: bucket tabs, ownership filter, state chips, clocks) and the flag card (patterns 2, 3, 5 in one component: reason line, one-line read-in, the draft as an SMS bubble, Approve / Edit, side-effect chips). Those two encode rules 1 through 6 and are enough for shadow mode to have a place to land. The scoreboard (pattern 10) only becomes real once shadow mode produces data, and the teaching loop (pattern 11) rides on the flag card anyway. Katie is building her own version this week; this page is the shared evidence base to compare both designs against.
